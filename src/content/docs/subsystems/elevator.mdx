The elevator carries the wrist from just above the frame to the top of the reef. It is the tallest and heaviest mechanism on the robot, so its speed, its motor current and the robot's balance all had to be worked out before anything was built.

<RobotViewer title="The elevator in 3D" sub="Elevator" />

Press L2, L3 or L4 under the model to watch the stages move.

## What we decided

| Choice | What we picked |
| --- | --- |
| Type | Three stages that slide inside each other, each moving 22 in |
| Travel | The carriage moves 66 in. The wrist pivot goes from 24.5 in to 90.5 in off the floor |
| Structure | 2 × 1 in aluminium tube with 1/16 in walls, on WCP bearing blocks. A diagonal brace on each side ties the mast back to the frame |
| Drive | Two Kraken X60 motors through a 12:1 gearbox, turning 22-tooth sprockets for #25 chain |
| Coming down | Gravity. There is no cable pulling it down |

## Why

- **Three stages.** The robot has to start under 42 in tall and still place a coral on L4, 72 in up, so the lift has to more than double its own height.
- **An existing pattern.** The layout follows the WCP GreyT three-stage elevator, so the bearing blocks are off-the-shelf parts.
- **No down-haul** saves a rope run and a tensioner. The cost is that the elevator cannot be driven down faster than it falls.
- **12:1** gives a free speed of 137.5 in/s, so the elevator can cruise at 110 in/s, 80% of that. With time to speed up and slow down, full travel takes about 1.1 s.

### How one drive moves three stages

Only the first moving stage is driven. The other two are pulled along by it.

1. **Chain lifts the first stage.** A chain on each side runs between a drive sprocket at the bottom of the mast and an idler at the top. The first stage is clamped to it.
2. **A rope lifts the second stage.** It is anchored at the bottom of the mast, runs up over a pulley at the top of the first stage, and down to the second stage. When the first stage rises 1 in, the pulley rises 1 in and the second stage rises 2 in.
3. **A rope lifts the carriage** the same way, over a pulley at the top of the second stage. So the carriage rises 3 in.

That is why the speed calculator multiplies by three, and why the holding calculator counts the carriage's weight three times. Each rope ends in an eye bolt with two nuts, which is how it is tightened.

The elevator finds its zero with a magnetic switch at the bottom. Printed rubber pads take the weight when it is down.

### What changed along the way

- The mast moved back, to 4.5 in ahead of the robot's centre, because the front swerve modules were in the way.
- The inside corners of the plates were opened to a 0.13 in radius so a 1/4 in end mill can cut them.
- The diagonal brace was riveted to the upright. Before that it was bolted at the foot and only touched the upright at the top, so it carried no load.
- A second brace was added on the left. There had only ever been one, on the right (see the worked example below).

### How much current does it take to hold still?

Holding a load in the air takes current even when nothing is moving. If holding takes most of what the motor is allowed, nothing is left to speed up.

<Calc id="elevator-hold" />

The motors are limited to 60 A each to start with. Holding takes about 19 A, so roughly two thirds of the limit is left for speeding up.

### How hard can the robot accelerate with the elevator up?

Raising the elevator raises the robot's centre of gravity, and a tall robot tips more easily.

| Pose | Centre of gravity height | Largest safe acceleration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stowed | 9.6 in | 1.07 g |
| Carriage halfway, wrist level | 15.7 in | 0.71 g |
| Carriage at the top, wrist level | 21.5 in | 0.52 g |

<Calc id="tip-accel" />

So the code limits how hard the robot can accelerate whenever the carriage is high.


### Worked example: does the mast need a brace, and on which side?

There was a diagonal brace on the right side of the mast and none on the left. The question was: do we need a brace at all, and if we do, why only one? The physics is the same on both sides. Here is how to answer it with hand sums. The calculator below does the same sums.

**1. Pick the load.** When the robot stops hard, or gets hit, everything on the elevator tries to keep going. Work it out for 1 g of sideways acceleration (one robot weight, pushing sideways), then scale it: a hard stop or a hit can be a few g. Do the worst case, with the carriage at the top.

**2. Work on one upright.** There are two uprights, so each takes half the load.

| What | Weight | Height above the mast base | Sideways force on one upright, at 1 g |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carriage and wrist | 19.8 lb | 85.4 in | 9.9 lb |
| Stage B | 4.7 lb | 62.6 in | 2.35 lb |
| Stage A | 5.7 lb | 39.75 in | 2.85 lb |

**3. Move the loads to the top of the mast.** The mast is 35 in tall, but the stages stick up above it. So at the mast top there is a sideways force and a twisting moment:

- Force V = 9.9 + 2.35 + 2.85 = **15.1 lb**
- Moment M = 9.9 × (85.4 − 35) + 2.35 × (62.6 − 35) + 2.85 × (39.75 − 35) = **577 lb·in**

**4. How stiff is the tube?** For a rectangular tube bending the deep way:

I = (width × depth³ − (width − 2 × wall) × (depth − 2 × wall)³) ÷ 12 = (1 × 2³ − 0.875 × 1.875³) ÷ 12 = **0.186 in⁴**

Aluminium has E = 10,000,000 psi, so EI = 1,860,000 lb·in².

**5. No brace.** The upright is a cantilever: held at the bottom, free at the top. The textbook formulas for a cantilever are:

- the top moves V × L³ ÷ (3EI) + M × L² ÷ (2EI) = 0.116 + 0.190 = **0.31 in**
- the top tilts V × L² ÷ (2EI) + M × L ÷ EI = **0.016 rad** (0.91°)

The carriage is 50 in above the mast top and leans with it, so it moves 0.31 + 0.016 × 50.4 = **1.1 in per g**. The tilt does most of the damage, not the bend.

The biggest bending moment is at the base: V × L + M = 1,106 lb·in. Stress = moment ÷ (I ÷ half the depth) = 1,106 ÷ 0.186 = **about 5,950 psi**. The aluminium is good to about 35,000 psi, so it is **strong enough**. Strength was never the problem.

**6. How wobbly is it?** A mass on a spring wobbles at √(386 ÷ sway at 1 g) ÷ 2π times a second (386 is g in inches per second squared). With 1.1 in of sway that is about **3 Hz**, slow enough to see. Every stop sets the carriage swinging, and the camera on the wrist swings with it.

**7. With a brace.** The brace joins the upright 27.25 in up. Treat it as a solid prop: the upright is now held at the base and pinned at 27.25 in.

- Moment in the upright at the brace: Mb = V × (35 − 27.25) + M = **694 lb·in**
- The upright turns there by Mb × 27.25 ÷ (4EI) = 0.0025 rad (a beam held at one end, pinned at the other, with a moment on the pinned end)
- Add the 7.75 in above the brace as a short cantilever, then lean the carriage as before: about **0.29 in per g**

That is a quarter of the unbraced sway. Wobble goes up to about 5.8 Hz, and the peak stress drops to about 3,750 psi.

The brace has to push back with V + 1.5 × Mb ÷ 27.25 = 53 lb sideways. It leans at an angle, so the push along it is 53 × 28.0 ÷ 10.5 = **142 lb**. A 1 × 1 × 1/16 tube 28 in long buckles at about 4,300 lb, so it has plenty of margin even at several g.

**8. One brace or two?** With a brace on one side only, the braced upright moves 0.29 in and the other moves 1.1 in. The stages ride on both uprights, so the mast has to twist: (1.1 − 0.29) ÷ 25 in between the uprights = 0.032 rad, about **1.9° per g**. That twist pinches the bearing blocks and the stages bind. So it is **both sides or neither**. The second brace and its gusset cost 0.85 lb.

**What we checked it against.** A computer beam model of the same mast, with the brace allowed to stretch a little, gave 1.11 in unbraced, 0.33 in braced, 5,950 psi, 3.0 and 5.5 Hz, 138 lb in the brace and 1.8° of twist. The hand sums are within about 10%: close enough to make the decision.

**The lesson.** The mast was strong enough without a brace, but it was floppy. For tall mechanisms, check how far they move and how fast they wobble, not just whether they break. And when something only exists on one side of a symmetric robot, ask why.

## What went wrong

**The mast had one brace, on the right, from the first layout on.** Nobody asked why until the design was nearly done. The sums above showed that one brace twists the mast, so the left brace was added.

**For a while the code had to run the elevator at 65 in/s, not the 110 in/s it was designed for.** At 110 in/s in simulation, the motion controller stopped slowing down in time and the carriage reached the top stop.

The cause was a units setting. The motor controller was asked to work in inches through a conversion ratio below 1, and its simulator did not handle that well. The fix was to let the controller work in motor turns and convert to inches in our own code. The elevator now runs at 110 in/s in simulation.

The lesson: a mechanism can be fast enough on paper and still be held back by how it is controlled. Run it in simulation at full speed before trusting the paper number.

For a new game:

- [ ] Find the lowest and highest point the mechanism has to reach, and the starting height limit. That sets the number of stages.
- [ ] Weigh, or model, everything that rides on each stage.
- [ ] Use the speed calculator to pick a gear ratio. Aim to cruise at about 80% of free speed.
- [ ] Use the holding calculator. Check that holding leaves most of the current limit free for speeding up.
- [ ] Find the centre of gravity with the elevator fully up, and work out the acceleration limit.
- [ ] Use the brace calculator. Check how far the top moves and how fast it wobbles, not only the stress. Brace both sides or neither.
